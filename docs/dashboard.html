1 · The market splits in two, and volume hides it

pc_ratio           = prompt tokens / completion tokens   → context consumed per token produced
tokens_per_request = total tokens / requests             → size of one interaction

Weighted by token volume, both axes are bimodal: modes at a P:C of 17 and 76, and at 10k and 62k tokens per request. The dashed cuts sit at the density minima between them, so they are measured, not chosen.

Why both axes. The highest P:C ratio in the dataset belongs to a safety classifier. It reads 195 tokens per token written, but its interactions average 726 tokens. On that axis alone a context-hungry classifier and a coding agent look identical.

The sticker price is not the price

Traffic is overwhelmingly prompt-heavy, and prompt tokens are cheaper than completions. So the price a buyer actually pays per token, blended across their real mix, sits at a median of 0.35× the headline output price — the sticker overstates the true unit cost by about 2.9×.

3 · The context window arms race is mostly unused

Vendors compete hard on advertised context length. Dividing the mean tokens per request by the advertised window asks how much of it the traffic touches.

The workloads bought for long context leave nine tenths of it idle. One caveat, cutting one way: tokens per request is a mean, so a model that fills a huge window occasionally and stays small usually reads low here. Typical usage, not peak capability.

5 · Price response, and where it hides

Log tokens on log price, with heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, under two weightings: one model one vote, or one request one vote.

The result worth the space is the reversal in agentic traffic. Counting models equally, price explains nothing. Weighting by requests, the elasticity is -0.37 (-0.65 to -0.10) and clears zero comfortably. Agentic models are not price-sensitive; agentic volume is, which is what a small number of very large consumers optimising unit cost looks like.

Association, not cause. No model is observed at two prices here. Cheap models differ from dear ones in quality as well as price, so a steep slope fits "buyers hunt cheap tokens" and "cheap models are built for bulk work" equally well. The contrast between archetypes survives that ambiguity; a single coefficient does not.

6 · How long does a model live? (preliminary)

Kaplan-Meier with right-censoring. Most models are still running, so their lifetime is known only to be at least their current age. Dropping them biases the curve towards short lives; counting their age as a lifetime biases it the other way. The product-limit estimator uses what each subject carries.

Death defined asEventsCensoredAlive at 180dAlive at 365d
≥2 days silent7141092.0%84.6%
≥3 days silent7141092.0%84.6%
≥7 days silent5242993.5%88.7%
≥14 days silent2845394.6%93.3%

Why this is preliminary. Death is inferred from the last day with traffic, and two biases pull against each other. A model silent for more than about 30 days leaves the monthly window entirely, so long-dead models are absent and survival is biased up. The tell is in the table above: a 14-day threshold finds almost no events, which is a property of the feed and not of the market. Meanwhile a 2-day threshold books a model that had a quiet Tuesday as dead, biasing down. Their relative sizes are unknown.

The fix costs only time. Once the archive holds several weeks of captures, death is observed, present on day N and absent on day N+k, instead of inferred from a truncated field. The estimator does not change; its input stops being biased.

For models shipping both tiers, the free variant carries a median 96% of the tokens, and its interactions run larger than the paid tier's. A funnel would look the opposite: small free trials graduating into heavy paid usage. This looks like substitution.

What this cannot tell you

The limits are part of the result.

  • No public history exists. The rankings feed returns trailing aggregates, and its date column is the model's last day with traffic, not a time index. Grouping by it produces a convincing and entirely false chart. Every series here starts on the day of the first capture.
  • Implied value is not revenue. List price × tokens, ignoring cache discounts, batch pricing, BYOK and negotiated rates. An upper bound.
  • Endpoint percentiles are a 30-minute sample, not a daily aggregate.
  • Four schema fields are dormant — cache, reasoning and tool-call counters are present but zero for every model. Nothing is built on them; a contract test watches for them waking up.
  • Traffic is not users. One agentic application can outproduce a million chat sessions. Nothing here measures adoption or satisfaction.
  • Archetype cuts are frozen, not re-fitted. Boundary models will flip between captures; that is measured rather than assumed away.
